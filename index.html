How much of the internet is original writing, and how much is the scanned past?

The internet is overwhelmingly made of text written for the internet. Everything humanity printed before it — every book, every newspaper, every scientific paper, from Gutenberg to 1995 — is a rounding error by comparison, and the part actually readable online is smaller still. But the size of that gap swings by a factor of six hundred depending on two definitional choices nobody surfaces, and the reason so little of the printed past is online turns out to have nothing to do with scanning technology.

A scale check that makes this concrete. One monthly Common Crawl snapshot yields about 20 TB of extracted plain text. All the OCR text in HathiTrust’s 19 million scanned volumes comes to about 15 TB. The web produces more text every three weeks than the largest academic book-digitisation project on earth has accumulated in two decades.

Where the printed past disappears

Two chokepoints stand between text that exists in print and text a person can read online. The first is scanning. The second is law. The second is the larger of the two, and it is the one that gets ignored.

The bottleneck is legal, not technical. Google has run 40 million books through its scanners and releases bulk text for none of them — not even the public-domain ones. HathiTrust will give researchers 5.4 TB of public-domain page text, but only under a signed agreement, locked to a single IP address, non-commercial, with a deletion obligation. Newspapers.com holds over a billion pages behind a subscription. The machines finished their work; the rights did not.

The printed past is mostly newspapers, not books

Discussions of digitising the past are almost entirely about books. But newspapers plausibly hold two to four times as much text as every book ever published — and they are the least accessible category of all.

Newspapers.com alone holds about as much text as every book HathiTrust has ever scanned — over a billion pages — and none of it is on the open web. The Library of Congress’s Chronicling America, the one fully open newspaper corpus, is 23 million pages: 2.3% of Newspapers.com by page count, and about one part in six hundred of all the newspaper text ever printed.

Treat the newspaper figure as an order of magnitude, not a measurement. No scholarly estimate of total newspaper pages ever printed appears to exist; this is derived from title counts, issue frequency and page counts, integrated over roughly two centuries. The per-page conversion is solid — 3,313 tokens per page, measured directly from OCR of 20 million real pages — but the page count it multiplies is not.

Why the answer swings by 600×

Two choices determine the number you get, and neither is usually stated. Which tier of pre‑internet text you count, and which point in the web estimate’s very wide confidence interval you compare it against. Every cell below is defensible from published figures.

Top-left to bottom-right is a 623× range, and both corners are honestly sourced. Within that, the tier choice is worth 39× and the web estimate choice is worth 16×. Anyone quoting a single ratio for this question has silently made both choices on your behalf. The most defensible framing is to state the tier explicitly and carry the web interval: freely readable pre‑internet text is 0.08%–1.35% of the born‑digital web, most likely around 0.34%.

Why raw byte counts mislead in both directions

Almost every large number in this field measures a container rather than its contents. The errors run in opposite directions on the two sides of the comparison, which is what makes naive ratios so unreliable.

  • On the web side, raw crawl bytes overstate text by ~200:1. A monthly Common Crawl snapshot is 424 TiB of raw HTTP content. Extracting plain text leaves ~20 TB. Filtering and deduplicating leaves 1.5–2.5 TB. Quoting the 424 TiB as “text on the web” overstates by more than two orders of magnitude.
  • On the print side, page images overstate text by 30–80×. HathiTrust’s 791 TB of storage contains about 15 TB of OCR text. Anna’s Archive’s 1.1 PB of files holds roughly 34 TB of text. Lyman & Varian’s influential figure of 208 TB for the Library of Congress is scanned page images; the text is closer to 13 TB.
  • Deduplication is not one number, it is two. Removing duplicates within a single web crawl strips about 44%. Removing them across eleven years of crawls strips 89%. The FineWeb team found the 89% version discarded the better data — well-maintained pages get re-crawled, so aggressive global deduplication preferentially deletes them — and shipped the 44% version instead.
  • “300 billion pages” is not 300 billion pages. Common Crawl’s cumulative figure counts page captures across snapshots; roughly half of each crawl is a re-fetch of a URL seen before.

What nobody has measured

Three gaps sit directly underneath this question, and each would change the answer if filled.

  • How many times canonical public-domain works appear on the web. No study counts this. It matters because raw byte counts of pre‑internet text online are inflated by redundancy: a Gutenberg Hamlet is 180 KB, but replicated across, say, five hundred sites it contributes ~90 MB of crawl. The canonical core — Shakespeare, the King James Bible, Austen, Dickens, the Holmes canon — is perhaps 50–100 MB of unique text occupying an unknown but far larger share of raw crawl bytes. This is a paper someone should write.
  • Total newspaper pages ever printed. The single largest quantity in this analysis has no scholarly estimate at all. The figure used here is derived, and it drives the “all print ever” total more than any other input.
  • Google Books’ accessibility breakdown. Google has never published how its 40 million scanned volumes divide between full view, preview, snippet and no-preview. That single unpublished table is the largest uncertainty in the “freely readable” tier.
  • Modern spam prevalence in web crawls. There is no credible recent measurement. Historical studies (2006–2011) put web spam at 6–22% of crawled pages; nothing comparable exists for the present.

Figures in circulation that don’t hold up

  • “Anna’s Archive holds 1.1 petabytes of books.” It holds 1.1 PB of files. 68.7 million books at ~500 KB of text each is about 34 TB — the PDF and DjVu containers inflate the text by roughly 32×. The same error applies to Library Genesis’s 320 TiB and HathiTrust’s 791 TB.
  • “Common Crawl contains 300 billion pages / the whole web.” Page captures, not distinct documents, and Common Crawl’s own engineer has said plainly that the claim it contains the entire web is “absolutely not true.” It is a centrality-weighted sample, heavily skewed toward English and high-PageRank pages.
  • “74% of new web pages are AI-generated” (Ahrefs, 2025). The measured claim is that 74.2% contain any AI-assisted text. The same study puts pure AI content at 2.5%. The detector is proprietary and undisclosed, and the vendor sells SEO tooling.
  • “90% of online content will be AI-generated by 2026” (Europol, 2022). A projection, not a measurement, and it did not happen. Best current evidence puts machine-generated content at roughly 30–50% of new articles since 2024, and single digits to ~15% of the cumulative web archive.
  • Kevin Kelly’s “all the works of humankind = 50 petabytes.” Accurate as stated, but it is over 99% audio, video and images. The text inside his list is about 35 TB.
  • “Google Books has digitised the world’s literature.” It has scanned 40 million of an estimated 129.9 million books — about 31% — and releases bulk text for none of it. The only text derivative Google ever published is the Ngram corpus, which is deliberately non-reconstructible counts, not readable text.

What to believe, graded

  • SolidBorn-digital web text vastly exceeds digitised pre-internet text. True under every combination of assumptions in the matrix above. The narrowest defensible gap is about 2:1; the widest about 1,200:1. The direction never reverses.
  • SolidThe barrier between scanned and readable is legal, not technical. Directly documented: Google releases no bulk text, HathiTrust requires a signed IP-locked agreement, Newspapers.com paywalls a billion pages.
  • SolidProject Gutenberg is ~0.0016% of the indexed web. Both sides precisely measured: 20.4 GB for 56,000 documents (Dolma’s measurement), against Epoch’s 1.75 PB.
  • ModerateFreely readable pre-internet text is ~0.3–0.4% of the web. Depends on the central web estimate, which carries a 16× confidence interval. Quote the range, not the point.
  • ModerateNewspapers hold more text than books. The per-page conversion is directly measured, but the total page count is derived and unverified.
  • WeakAny specific figure for “all text ever printed.” Four independent estimates converge within a factor of ~4, which is reassuring but not precision. The newspaper term dominates and is the least sourced.
  • UnsupportedAny claim about how much of the web is duplicated classics. Nobody has measured it. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise, in either direction.
